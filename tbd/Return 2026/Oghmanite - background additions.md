# Oghmanite: background additions

Proposed additions to Panagiotis's `OGHMANITE BACKGROUND`, written in his register so
they can be pasted in. **Every section here is a proposal.** His text stays as it is;
each addition says which of his sections it follows.

Rules I kept to:

- **The life, not the mysteries.** Nothing here explains the woman, the Missing
  Definition, the Nine, Lareth or the word in the dreams. Those stay his.
- **His placeholders stay his.** Where he wrote "θα βρω" or "θα σου πω", I offer an
  option in [brackets], never a fact.
- **Inspiration.** Coelho's *Manual of the Warrior of Light* shapes the inner man: his
  code, how he meets defeat, the blank book he writes in. Eco's *The Name of the Rose*
  and Pérez-Reverte's *The Club Dumas* shape his method and his scenes. Nothing is
  quoted from them; it is all retold.
- **No name yet.** Second person throughout, as in Panagiotis's text.

## 1. → after CHILDHOOD

*Option for Panagiotis's family placeholder. If he has other plans, drop this
section.*

**THE BINDERS' HOUSE:** [Option] You were born into a family of bookbinders in
[Berdusk, a street away from the Seat of Lore, or wherever suits]. Your earliest memories
smell of hide glue and wet leather. You learned the sound of a good press before you
learned your letters.

Your father had one rule, and he repeated it so often it became a joke in the house:
**"A binding tells you who loved a book. The text only tells you who wrote it."**

You were seven when you found your first secret. An old psalter came in for repair, and
when your father opened its boards you saw that they were not wood. They were paper:
dozens of old printed sheets pasted together and pressed hard, the way covers were made
long ago. One of those sheets carried a few lines of a text that existed nowhere else in
the city. Someone had thrown it away, and someone else had used it as stuffing.

It had survived because nobody thought it mattered.

You kept that sheet for years. You still think of it whenever someone tells you
something is lost.

## 2. → after YOUR MENTOR

*Panagiotis: the mentor is **a gnome**. If he is Bransuldyn Mirrortor of the Seat of
Lore (alive in 1160, which the 'final notes' complicate), this is a portrait of him: the
lenses, the jokes and the questions all fit.*

**WHAT HE WAS LIKE:** He was not the stern master you expected. He laughed easily, and
he used laughter as a tool. When a zealot contradicted himself, your mentor would point
it out so gently that the room laughed before the zealot understood why.

He wore **reading lenses** in a horn frame and was faintly embarrassed by them. He said
an old man who refuses lenses is choosing to read less, and that is the only sin a
scholar can commit against himself.

He taught you four things you still use every day.

**First: books are not to be believed. They are to be questioned.** A book is a
witness, not a judge. It can lie, be mistaken, or be telling a truth nobody has
understood yet.

**Second: keep a ledger of your errors.** Every theory you hold goes into a notebook.
When a theory dies, you don't erase it. You draw a single line through it and write
beside it why it died. "A crossed-out line still teaches," he said. "A scraped page
teaches nothing." You realised later that this was the only way a man sworn never to
destroy a book could keep a record of his own mistakes.

**Third: fear the man who would die for the truth more than the man who lies.** The
liar wants something, and you can find out what. The man in love with his own truth
will burn the library to keep it pure.

**Fourth: never lick your finger to turn a page.** He said it like a joke, and he
meant it. "Some books bite," he said. You have never asked what happened to the man
who taught him that.

**THE BELLS:** When you were stuck, he told you about a boy who spent a year on a beach
listening for the bells of a temple that had sunk beneath the sea. The boy heard
nothing, day after day, until the day he gave up and simply sat there, listening to the
waves for their own sake. Then he heard all the bells at once.

"You are listening too hard," your mentor would say. "Read something else for a week."

It always annoyed you. It was usually right.

## 3. → after THE LAST LESSON (or after "Your mentor's final note")

**THE BLANK BOOK:** Among the things your mentor left you were his horn-rimmed lenses,
which you now wear when the light is bad and never admit to needing, and a book. It was well bound,
in plain leather, with a scroll tooled on the cover, the symbol of Oghma. Every page was
**blank**.

There was no note with it. You understood anyway.

Oghma's symbol is a blank scroll. To most people it means knowledge not yet written. To
your mentor it meant *the duty to write it*.

So you began to write. Not research: that goes in your ledgers. This book is for
something else. After every hard day, every failure, every lesson that cost you
something, you write one short entry. Each begins the same way:

***A Shield of the Scroll…***

You have never shown it to anyone. These are some of the entries:

> *A Shield of the Scroll names a defeat as a defeat. He fought for the manuscripts at
> Asbravn and did not save them. He does not tell himself that something was saved
> after all.*

> *A Shield of the Scroll does not hide his scars. They are the only honest record of
> what happened in that temple, and he will not have them erased either.*

> *A Shield of the Scroll may let his body mislead, and his silence. Never his words.*

> *A Shield of the Scroll states his anger plainly, once. Then he puts it away and goes
> back to work.*

> *A Shield of the Scroll does not judge Corvus. He stops him, and restores what Corvus
> took. The judging belongs to Oghma.*

> *When a Shield of the Scroll cannot set right a wrong done to the dead, he does a
> kindness for a stranger and dedicates it, silently, to the one he failed.*

> *A Shield of the Scroll draws a line through a mistake. He never scrapes it off the
> page.*

> *A Shield of the Scroll teaches what he learned, and says whom he learned it from.
> That is how his master is still alive.*

> *When the trail goes quiet, a Shield of the Scroll grows more careful, not less. A
> library is never quieter than when something is hunting in it.*

> *A Shield of the Scroll once read that a knight's first lesson is to erase everything
> written in the book of his life. He has decided that it means: begin a new page.*

## 4. → after YOUR FIRST REAL FAILURE

*Panagiotis's text says the first encounter happened but doesn't tell it. A short
scene, if he likes it.*

**THE FIRST TIME:** It happened in [Zazesspur, in Tethyr], in the private library of an
old merchant house that had hired the Children to catalogue its collection before
selling it.

There was a young copyist working there that month. Polite. Quiet. Very good hands. You
spoke with him twice and remember nothing he said.

On your last day you checked the house's most valuable item: a chronicle of the
family's founding, with a genealogy at the back. **The binding was right. The paper was
right. One leaf was wrong.** It was a little too clean, and the ink sat on the surface
instead of biting into it.

Someone had removed a page and replaced it with a perfect copy, perfect except for one
name in the genealogy, which was no longer there.

You went to find the copyist. His room was empty. On his desk, neatly folded, was the
leaf he had taken out, and on it, drawn in fresh ink, **a skull above a dagger, framed
by curved horns.**

He hadn't stolen the page. He had left it for you.

That night the library burned. You woke to smoke, and got out through a window with as
many books in your arms as you could carry. The chronicle was not among them.

You survived. You did not understand why someone would go to such trouble to erase one
name, hand you the evidence, and then burn everything else. You still don't, entirely.
But you remember that the copyist smiled at you on the stairs that morning, and you
smiled back.

## 5. → after THE LEDGER OF GRACE

*This turns Panagiotis's "sometimes you're wrong" into the character's signature flaw,
with one worked example. It fits his text: you arrived too late.*

**THE PATTERN YOU BUILT:** You arrived too late, and it was your own fault.

For months before the plague village, you had tracked a series of thefts: three temples,
three sacred texts, three cities. You laid them out in your ledger and saw a pattern.
Every theft fell on the feast day of a saint, and every stolen book had belonged to that
saint's order. You were proud of it. It was elegant. It predicted the next target: a
shrine in [a city], on the coming feast day.

You went there and waited a week. Nothing happened.

Meanwhile, in a quarantined village, Corvus walked into a temple of Ilmater that fitted
no pattern at all.

Later you understood. The three thefts had been the work of three different hands, and
the "pattern" was yours. You had found order where there was only coincidence, because
order is what you are trained to find. And Corvus, you are fairly sure, learned what you
believed and let you go on believing it.

In your ledger, the theory still stands. Neatly crossed out. Beside it, in your hand:
*"Elegant. Wrong. Twelve dead."*

You still find patterns. You can't help it; it's what your mind does. But now, every
time you find one, you ask a second question: **is this the world's pattern, or mine?**
Sometimes you can't tell. That is the most frightening thing you know about yourself.

## 6. → after THE WOUND / THE YEARS AFTER ASBRAVN

**THE MORNING AFTER:** The night after Asbravn you couldn't stand, so you couldn't kneel.
You prayed anyway, lying on the floor of a farmhouse with your leg bound, and then you
took out the blank book and wrote that you had lost.

Not "nearly won". Not "saved what could be saved". **Lost.**

Your hands still carry the burns from the Ledger's fire. When you came back from
Tethyr, years before, the Children's own healers offered to take those scars away. You
refused. They are the only honest record of that night, and you will not let anyone take
them. You expect you will say the same about your leg.

Corvus smiled at you from the altar. You have thought about that smile more than you
would like to admit. Sometimes, on bad nights, it is what gets you out of bed. You have
written down, once, that this is anger, and that it is useful, and that it is not
revenge. You read that entry when you need to.

## 7. → after OTHER THINGS FROM THE PAST, A (Karran-Kul)

**CANDLEKEEP:** The two weeks at Irondagger became two months at Candlekeep.

The first time you saw **Nennul Exul** you were glad of your mentor's rule. You turned
its pages with a silver knife and a gloved hand, and never touched the leaves with bare
skin. Whether the stories about it are true, or the madness is only the obsession of
everyone who has wanted it, you don't know. You decided not to find out the hard way.

[If Candlekeep holds more than one account of the Seat; see Questions.] In the
restricted archives you found two accounts of the Seat that the tome describes.
**They disagreed.** Different names, a different binding, a different kind of fiend.
Someone had copied one from the other and changed things, or both came from a third
source that no longer exists. You haven't settled which. It is on your list.

## 8. → after OTHER THINGS FROM THE PAST, B (the Harpers)

**ORIANNA:** She was a bard before she was ever your contact. You met her
[when and where: Panagiotis's call] and argued with her for an entire evening about
whether a song can be a historical source.

She won.

Her argument was simple. A chronicle can be burned. A song gets sung in a thousand
taverns by people who don't know what it's about. And when everyone has forgotten the
words, the tune is still there. You've thought about that ever since: the tune is
the last thing to die.

You were not there when she was killed. You found out from a letter, three weeks late.

You have written her name in the blank book. Under it, nothing. You haven't found the
words yet, and you won't put down the wrong ones.

## 9. → after ΤΕΛΕΥΤΑΙΑ ΑΠΟΣΤΟΛΗ – ΤΡΕΧΟΥΣΑ ΘΕΣΗ

**WHY YOU STAYED:** Nadari told you the mission was complete. You agreed, because it
was. Then you stayed, and you told her why, because you cannot lie:

The lair beyond the portal was a shrine of St. Selimbrar, the saint of Pylacheus and of
the Ledger's martyrs. Corvus's symbol was on its wall. Corvus serves an assassin who
may now be a Chosen of the risen Lord of Murder. A girl in Teziir dreams of nine candles
going out, one by one.

And Orianna died a year ago, and nobody has told you how.

You told Nadari all of it except the last part. That one you kept, by saying nothing.
It is the only way you know how to keep a thing.
