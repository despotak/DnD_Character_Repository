# Selanar's Journal — Heroes of Faerun (Crusty's Cave)

## Entry I — The Fall of Myth Drannor

*Semberholme, 1487 DR. The fourth day, I am assured, with a confidence I do not share.*

---

My coat is ruined.

I record it first because it is the only loss in this camp that anyone will permit me to discuss. Tyrian silk, cut in Leuthilspar by a man who wept when he took my measurements, and it now carries a hole the size of my fist and a stain I decline to describe in a book my brother may one day read. The healers *cut it off me*. Cut it. There were seams. I have raised the matter of the seams with three separate people and not one of them has been what I would call moved.

The tent smells of cedar and old blood. The food is a grey business they are calling broth, which I have chosen to interpret as a courtesy. There is a healer here named Aeliana with a very fine jaw who has informed me twice that I am not to write and once that I am the worst patient in the wood — which, in the circumstances, I have elected to receive as flirtation. A man must have projects.

Everyone in this wood is being extremely careful with me, and I have worked out why. It is not the wound.

---

They have forbidden me the pen on the grounds that the wound will open. Let it open.

I have spent my life reading accounts composed by men who waited until they were comfortable, and I have never trusted a word of one of them. If I do not set this down now, while it is still ugly, I shall begin to arrange it — and a month from now I shall possess a fine and creditable account of a battle I did not fight, in a hand steady enough to be believed.

I am aware that I have opened with tailoring. I am aware of precisely what I am doing. I intend to go on doing it for another page or so, and then I intend to stop.

---

Our father raised us on the fall of cities.

Aryvandaar, and the pride that ate it from within. Old Cormanthyr. And Myth Drannor — the first Myth Drannor, the one that died seven centuries before I drew breath — recited at our bedside as the last word on the subject: that the mighty fall, and that they fall precisely because they have grown certain that they cannot. Taenis slept through it. I never did.

I thought it was a story about other people.

I have now had it twice in one lifetime, once in my father's voice and once with my own eyes, and I record for whatever the observation is worth that the second telling omits nothing the first contained. Pride, numbers, and a sky that opens.

---

Now. The story.

I have been practising it, because one must. In eighty years I shall be expected to tell it at table, and a man who tells a bad war story is worse than a man who tells none at all. Let me set down the version I have been polishing, and we shall both see how far it gets.

The Netherese did not come as an army. An army answers to a homeland and keeps a discipline; an army can be shamed. They came as bought steel — *N'Tel'Quess* beyond counting, butchers for coin, not one of them with the least stake in the ground he was taking — and the towers burned above us for three days.

By the end of the third there were eight of us left, the Coronal among us.

Ilsevele Miritar, *Cor'Etriel*, who did not once in three days give an order in a raised voice. Fflar Starbrow Melruth, who died for this city seven centuries ago and came back for the encore, and who appeared to regard the second occasion as an administrative matter. Storm Silverhand, away to the right, doing something appalling to a company of Sembian pikemen and showing no sign of ever having been taught how to be tired. Three knights of the Akh'Faer. Taenis at my left shoulder, where he has stood since the practice yards at the Glade.

And your correspondent: Akh'Faer of four entire months, which is to say a scholar who had been given a place in a line and told where to stand in it — holding a sword like a man holding someone else's baby.

Then they closed on us, and the ring drew in, and I felt the particular arithmetic of it: the way one knows, without troubling to count, that the sum will not come out in one's favour.

And then my hands caught fire.

---

Here one pauses. Here the table leans in.

Silver-blue and silent as snowfall, azure at the root and at the edges every colour that light can hold, pouring down my arm and out of my open hand with no formula, no gesture, no somatic component, nothing of the Art as the Art was taught to me — only a snarl, and the reflex of a blade, and a hundred and twelve years of not knowing the thing was in me.

It was beautiful. I am aware how that reads, written four days after the death of my city, and I decline to strike it out. I have argued my whole life that what is beautiful points past itself toward what is true, and I will not now pretend I failed to notice the colour of what came out of me.

It made no sound. That is the detail I keep returning to, and the one I most want explained to me. Fire of that magnitude ought to roar. It did not. It went about its work in perfect silence, as though sound were a courtesy it did not owe us.

*Spellfire*, I am since told the word is. I did not have it then. I knew only that I was burning and was not being consumed.

And then, with the eyes of legends upon me and my city coming apart above my head, I threw it.

And missed.

---

That is the story. That is the entire story. He *stepped aside*.

A sellsword. No one. A chipped pauldron and the bored economical footwork of a man who has done this in four countries — he stepped politely out of the way of the miracle and came in under my guard while I stood admiring my own palms like a child who has broken something in his mother's house.

I felt it go deep. There was no pain worth the name at first, which I had read was common and did not believe until it was done to me. The world narrowed, and filled with red, and then black came crawling in at the edges of the red; and I remember thinking, with tremendous calm, that this was *a poor first showing for a gift so long in coming*.

It is a very good story. I have told it to myself perhaps forty times since I woke.

I notice that it stops being funny at a different place each time.

---

Last night it stopped at Fflar. Tonight it stops earlier.

There were three knights of the Akh'Faer in that ring whose names I never learned. Four months I stood among them; three days I stood *beside* them, close enough to be splashed; and I cannot tell you what to carve.

I have been composing an amusing anecdote about the afternoon those men died in, and I cannot supply their names.

Less amusing than advertised, then. I said I would stop. Here is where I stop.

---

Then she was beside me.

A small elf, no taller than my shoulder. Sapphire hair moving as though she stood in a wind arranged for her alone, dark eyes lit from within, and in her hand a sword that was not entirely present and that my eye kept declining to hold. She swept it through the air and the air came apart, and the sellswords with it: flesh, bone, armour and steel cloven alike, as though the distinction between those four things had never been more than a polite fiction we had all agreed to observe.

Someone said a name. I believe it was Fflar. *Srinshee.*

I have searched since, as far as a man may search from a cot, and turned up fragments that decline to agree with one another. That she drew the Rulers' Blade in the year 666 and refused the throne it offered her. That she returned after seven centuries of absence and put that same blade into Ilsevele's hands. She is spoken of the way one speaks of weather, or of gods, or of both, and in the same breath as events four hundred years apart. I do not know what she is. I am not confident the word *who* survives contact with her.

She looked at me. At my chest, at my hand, at the thing that had come out of it. Every examination I ever sat at the Lady's College, every viva before the Silver Twilight — and I have never in my life been *known* like that. It took less than a breath.

I do not think she was even curious.

---

Then she turned. Her face blurred, or my eyes did; I cannot separate the two and have stopped trying. The dark deepened — not nightfall, but something with weight in it, something descending — and I understood, in the last clear moment I was given, that the floating city was coming down on all our heads.

And then, between one blink and the next: green.

There was no interval. None. There was Myth Drannor, and then there was Semberholme — wet cedar in the air, ground under me instead of pavement, no spires, no fire, no butchers-for-coin. Elves everywhere in bloodied armour, standing in the leaf-mould, weeping and taking hold of one another. The last of the Myth Drannans, gathered in a wood, about a Coronal of nowhere.

Three breaths of it, perhaps. Then the red came back and took me.

---

I woke here. The Coronal lives. Taenis lives.

He has been in and out of this tent so often that the healers have begun addressing him as furniture. He came this morning, looked at my right forearm, said nothing whatsoever, and went away again — and Taenis has never in his life said nothing whatsoever, so I now know precisely how bad it looks.

The mark is fading. The one that surfaced unbidden when I was a boy weaving my first *dweomercræft*, the one about which every elf I have ever met has held a strong opinion, has gone grey and soft at the edges, like something left too long in the sun.

And the fire is gone.

I have reached for it four days running. It is not there. Not refusal — refusal wants something present to do the refusing. I go to the place where it was, and I find the place, and it is empty, and it is the particular emptiness of a room whose occupant has stepped out. Even the ordinary Art comes thin and slow now, like a man talking through a wall.

---

So. An inventory, in place of a conclusion.

A city, gone. A coat, gone. A gift that arrived in the final hour and was away by morning, having achieved precisely nothing but my own near-death. A woman who opened the world like a seam and looked at me for a quarter of a second. Three names I owe and cannot pay. And a wound they assure me will close.

I am aware that this entry has not gone the way I intended. I began it about tailoring.

I am told I shall walk within the month. I am told to rest, to be grateful, and above all to stop pestering the sages concerning her — of whom I have now asked three, and two priests besides. Two of them laughed at me. One went the colour of paper and has since arranged never to be alone in a room with me.

I shall be asking again tomorrow.

I have a coat to replace and a question to answer, and of the two — to my very considerable annoyance — I find that I mind the question more.

---

*Signed in full, at length, and with all titles — a practice I have always held to be vulgar, and which I intend to indulge tonight for reasons I decline to examine.*

**Selanar Durothil**

Second son of Arandurian of House Durothil and of Iamorasseianna of House Aluviirsan

Scion of Tintageer and of Aryvandaar — *two realms of unmatched glory, both of them extinct; I am given to understand this is a family tradition*

Loyal subject of Cor'Etriel Ilsevele of House Miritar — *Coronal of nowhere, long may she reign over it*

Akh'Faer of Cormanthyr — *four months, of which three were spent losing*

Aegiess of Myth Drannor — *ask me what I am guarding. Go on. Ask.*

Seeker of Mysteries

*I observe that the last is the only title on this list to have survived the week.*

*It is also the only one I gave myself.*

*Aeliana has just taken the pen. I have another.*

## Entry II — An Errand for My Brother

*Aboard a trade barge on the Semberflow, the 2nd of Eleint, 1501 DR. Written by a very small lamp, in a hold that smells of onions.*

---

It took me eleven years to replace the coat.

I set that down before anything else, because I closed the last of these books on the observation that I had a coat to replace and a question to answer, and I am delighted to report that in fourteen years I have managed precisely one of the two. The coat is charcoal. It is very fine. It is at this moment folded in the bottom of a bag beneath a quantity of undyed wool, because I am travelling *incognito*, and a man in good tailoring is a man who gets remembered.

I have been reduced to homespun. Fourteen years of grief and scholarship, and the tale finds me in a garment the colour of wet mice.

---

The question, since you ask, remains open.

Fourteen years I have spent within a day's walk of Semberholme, reading everything that survived and a good deal that ought not to have, and I can now tell you a very great deal about the Weave and almost nothing about what happened to my hand. The mark came back — slowly, the way a bruise comes back, across the second year and the third — and the Art came back with it, and I applied myself to the rekindling with the discipline of a man who has nothing else on.

The full results, after fourteen years of unbroken effort:

I can clean my cuffs without water. I can light a candle across a room. I can bring a bottle of wine to precisely the temperature I prefer it, which I maintain is not nothing.

Prestidigitation. A hundred and twenty-six years old, handed a miracle in the last hour of my city, and I have made myself *exceptional at tidying*.

---

The fire is there. I want to be careful how I write even this.

It is there. I have felt it twice — both times when I was not looking at it, which appears to be the trick, and is a maddening sort of trick to be handed. It has not gone. It has merely declined to be summoned, the way a cat declines, and I have established beyond doubt that the surest method of not seeing it is to want to.

What I have not done is mention this to anybody.

Not the sages, who laughed. Not the Court, which has grief enough without being handed a curiosity. Not the two extremely courteous people who came asking questions in the fifth year and were, I could not help noticing, very interested indeed in my hand. Taenis knows, because Taenis was there, and because there has never been the least point in lying to him.

That is the entire list.

I have read enough history to know what becomes of the man who is the only one of something.

---

Now. The errand.

A squire came for me yesterday at an hour when I was not expecting to be found, concerning which I shall say nothing further except that the boy was very young and has my sympathy.

*Your brother needs to speak with you. It is urgent.*

Two observations on that sentence. First, Taenis has not used the word *urgent* to me in fourteen years; he regards urgency as a failure of planning. Second, the boy delivered it in the tone of someone who had been made to learn it by heart.

I found him in the great hall. Alone. Which is a great deal worse than finding him with the household.

House Starym are digging.

Outside Moontassel, in Sembia — comfortably beyond the wood, comfortably beyond anyone's authority, and comfortably far from anyone inclined to ask them what they imagine they are doing. They are looking for something. Taenis does not know what. He knows that they have committed people and coin to it, and that they have been *quiet* about it, and that House Starym are never quiet concerning anything they intend to share.

"If they come up with it," he said, "we are on the back foot. And we do not recover from the back foot. Not with them."

He cannot send a knight; a knight is a statement. He cannot send an agent, because he does not trust the agents he has — which is its own conversation, and one I fully intend to have with him on my return.

So he is sending his brother. A scholar. A man who has not been past the treeline in fourteen years and cannot reliably light a candle in a wind.

I said yes before he had finished asking, obviously.

---

We settled the cover between us in under a minute, which I found faintly insulting.

The story is that I have gone into the library. That I am not to be disturbed. That I am pursuing something in the older stacks and may be some months about it.

Not one person has questioned this. Not one. The steward nodded. The archivist offered to have meals sent down. Aeliana — senior healer these nine years, and still pretending with great conviction that she does not enjoy my company — said, and I quote in full: *"Well. Of course you have."*

I have been the sort of man who disappears into a library for a season for so long that I have accidentally built the perfect alibi out of my own personality. I am not entirely certain how to feel about that. I have elected to feel clever about it, because the other option is available and I would rather not.

---

I left at twilight with next to nothing, which is the part I resent most.

A man ought to prepare. A man ought to have a fortnight, a list, and a satisfying argument with a quartermaster. I had an afternoon. I have my components, such as they are; my books; the drab wool; the good coat I am forbidden to wear; and my longsword, which I have not drawn since the third day of the siege.

I notice that I packed it without hesitating, and without once looking directly at it. Fourteen years it has hung on a wall being decorative, and I have walked past it perhaps ten thousand times. I could not tell you tonight whether I brought it because I may have need of it, or because leaving it behind would have been a statement I was not prepared to make.

I did consider piloting myself downriver. I got as far as picturing it — the solitude, the self-sufficiency, the romance of the thing — and then I pictured explaining to Taenis how I came to be *in* the Semberflow rather than upon it, and I paid a bargeman four silver instead.

He is called Hobb. He has not asked me a single question, which I first took for discretion and now understand to be an absolute absence of interest. It is the most restful company I have kept in years.

The route, recorded here because if anyone ever reads this book it will be somebody trying to work out where I went: down the Semberflow while it will have me, then up the Glaemril, then Highmoon — where I mean to attach myself to a caravan bound for Ordulin, or passing near enough to it that I can be extremely boring about my reasons.

I travel as a copyist. Aldreth, out of Highmoon: no house, no titles, indifferent prospects, seeking work with any merchant company wanting its ledgers kept in a fair hand. It is a superb cover, for the single reason that it is very nearly true — and I have discovered that the easiest lie to maintain is the one you would have to make an effort *not* to tell.

I wanted a better name. I proposed three. Taenis rejected all three on the grounds that they were, in his words, "names for a man who wants to be looked at." He has known me since we were boys and he fights extraordinarily dirty.

---

So: a river, a caravan, a hole in the ground in Sembia, and a rival house digging in it.

I ought to be frightened. I have spent fourteen years being quiet and careful and useful in a library, and I am now going into Sembia alone, under a name that is not mine, carrying a sword I have not drawn in fourteen years, to spy upon people who have wanted my family dead since before my grandfather drew breath.

I notice, with considerable interest, that I am not frightened.

I notice that I have not felt this awake since the third day of the siege.

That is almost certainly a thing I should examine. I have decided to examine it later.

---

One further note, and then the lamp.

I have reached for it every day for fourteen years.

I reached tonight, out on the deck, with the black water going past and nobody at all watching — and for the first time in a very long while, I did not have to reach quite so far.

I am almost certainly imagining it.

I shall reach again tomorrow.

---

*Signed, as I must be for some months yet —*

**Aldreth**

*Copyist. Of Highmoon. No house, no titles, and nothing whatsoever worth looking at.*
